# Literature review: mutant screens as a phenotype assay for function roles

Compiled 2026-11-26. Topic-only search (no project details in queries).

**How verification was done.** Every entry below had its bibliographic record (title, authors, venue, year) confirmed by a fetched page: the Crossref API record for the DOI, the arXiv API record, a publisher/PMC/USENIX page, or a PMC page. Links given are DOIs (resolve via https://doi.org/<DOI>) or URLs I opened. Summaries are my own paraphrase. Entries marked (†) had their abstract paraphrased from my background knowledge of the paper (the record was verified, the abstract was not re-read); entries without a mark were summarised from a page I read. Items I could not verify are in the final "UNVERIFIED" list and are kept out of the executive summary. Limits of the search: ACM DL, DBLP and the DoomWiki were blocked to my fetcher, and the search tool is not Google Scholar, so recent (2025-26) preprints may be missed.

## Executive summary

1. Well established: mutant schemata (Untch et al. 1993), the equivalent-mutant problem, selective/sampled/higher-order mutation, and the coupling effect are mature; so are spectrum- and mutation-based fault localisation, differential testing of independent implementations, and execution-based ("dynamic") function similarity (Jiang and Su 2009; BLEX 2014; code relatives 2016).
2. Closest prior work on "pattern of failures caused by a mutant as a fingerprint": Metallaxis-FL and MUSE (fault localisation), which compare a real fault's failing-test pattern with each mutant's kill pattern inside ONE program; and Shin and Bae's d-vector framework, which formalises behavioural difference between program variants as a vector over tests.
3. Closest prior work on mutants across languages: Guizzo et al. 2023 (mutation-based translation analysis) mutate a Java program, translate the mutant to Python, and check the mutant and its translation agree. It evaluates translators; it does not match functions by lesion phenotype.
4. Closest biological template: phenologs (McGary et al. 2010) link phenotypes across species by significant overlap in their orthologous gene sets; ours would invert this (link genes/functions by shared phenotype). Nehrt et al. 2011 is the warning label (orthologs are not always the best functional matches).
5. Not found: any work that defines a function's role by its lesion phenotype and then matches functions across independent implementations. We searched mutation, fault-injection, clone-detection, binary-diffing and orthology vocabularies (see Novelty assessment).
6. Not found: use of mutant schemata as a phenotyping assay (the literature uses schemata only to cut cost); not found: a head-to-head of structure-based vs phenotype-based function matching with the orthology-benchmark mindset (Altenhoff et al. 2016 is the biological model).
7. Biggest methodological threats the literature flags: equivalent/unreached mutants masquerading as "no phenotype", mutant redundancy and subsumption inflating counts, test-suite power, flaky/nondeterministic outcomes, and unreliable ground truth in behavioural-similarity benchmarks (BigCloneBench audit).
8. DOOM-port-specific prior work is practitioner material only (DoomWiki, forums); no peer-reviewed study of desyncs or port evolution found. Fiedler's lockstep article gives the determinism requirements.
9. Honest novelty read: the combination is new to the extent searched, but each ingredient has strong precedent, so claims should be framed as "adapting forward genetics to cross-implementation role inference", with the baselines listed at the end.
10. Five papers to read first: (a) Papadakis and Le Traon, Metallaxis-FL (2013/2015); (b) Shin and Bae, d-vector framework (ICST 2016); (c) Guizzo et al., mutation analysis for evaluating code translation (EMSE 2023); (d) McGary et al., phenologs (PNAS 2010); (e) Jiang and Su, mining functionally equivalent code fragments (ISSTA 2009) together with Deissenboeck et al. on why that is hard (CSMR 2012).

## 1. Mutation testing foundations and cost reduction

### 1.1 Surveys and validity
- Jia, Y. and Harman, M. (2011) "An Analysis and Survey of the Development of Mutation Testing", IEEE TSE. https://doi.org/10.1109/tse.2010.62 . Comprehensive survey of techniques, operators, equivalent mutant handling and applications through 2009. (†) Why it matters: the reference map of the field.
- Papadakis, M., Kintis, M., Zhang, J., Jia, Y., Le Traon, Y., Harman, M. (2019) "Mutation Testing Advances: An Analysis and Survey", Advances in Computers vol. 112. https://doi.org/10.1016/bs.adcom.2018.03.015 (authors from the ORBilu/search record). Surveys 2009-2018 work on the core problems (cost, equivalence, operators, test generation). Why it matters: single best entry point for cost-reduction and equivalence literature.
- Just, R., Jalali, D., Inozemtseva, L., Ernst, M. D., Holmes, R., Fraser, G. (2014) "Are mutants a valid substitute for real faults in software testing?", FSE. https://doi.org/10.1145/2635868.2635929 . Finds mutant detection correlates with real fault detection, but a sizeable share of real faults is not coupled to any mutant. (†) Why it matters: bounds how far "lesion phenotype = what the code does" can be pushed with one-token mutants.
- Papadakis, M., Henard, C., Harman, M., Jia, Y., Le Traon, Y. (2016) "Threats to the validity of mutation-based test assessment", ISSTA. https://doi.org/10.1145/2931037.2931040 . Shows that results depend on duplicated/subsumed mutants and on how mutation scores are aggregated. (†) Why it matters: direct warning for any per-function phenotype counting.
- Chekam, T. T., Papadakis, M., Le Traon, Y., Harman, M. (2017) "An Empirical Study on Mutation, Statement and Branch Coverage Fault Revelation That Avoids the Unreliable Clean Program Assumption", ICSE. https://doi.org/10.1109/icse.2017.61 . Re-examines mutation-vs-coverage claims without assuming a perfect reference program. (†) Why it matters: treat our "reference" engine as fallible too.
- Petrovic, G., Ivankovic, M., Fraser, G., Just, R. (2022) "Practical Mutation Testing at Scale: A view from Google", IEEE TSE. https://doi.org/10.1109/tse.2021.3107634 ; arXiv https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.11378 . Describes a deployed system that samples and suppresses unproductive mutants at very large scale. (†) Why it matters: evidence that sampling and operator curation work at industrial scale.
- Petrovic, G., Ivankovic, M., Fraser, G., Just, R. (2021) "Does Mutation Testing Improve Testing Practices?", ICSE. https://doi.org/10.1109/icse43902.2021.00087 . Longitudinal Google study. (†) Marginal for us; context for mutation practice.

### 1.2 Mutant schemata and execution cost
- Untch, R. H., Offutt, A. J., Harrold, M. J. (1993) "Mutation analysis using mutant schemata", ISSTA. https://doi.org/10.1145/154183.154265 . Encodes many mutants into one compiled "meta-mutant" program with run-time selection, avoiding one compile per mutant. (†) Why it matters: it is exactly our build-once, switch-by-environment-variable design; cite as the origin, and note it was aimed at cost not at phenotyping.
- Just, R. (2014) "The Major mutation framework: efficient and scalable mutation analysis for Java", ISSTA (tool demo). https://doi.org/10.1145/2610384.2628053 . Compiler-integrated mutation generating a schema-style program with runtime activation. (†) Why it matters: a mature schemata implementation to compare engineering choices against.
- Just, R., Ernst, M. D., Fraser, G. (2014) "Efficient mutation analysis by propagating and partitioning infected execution states", ISSTA. https://doi.org/10.1145/2610384.2610388 . Shares execution among mutants whose state is identical after the mutation point. (†) Why it matters: gives a cheap way to detect mutants that are "silent" at the first state check, i.e. a built-in unreached/inert filter.
- Zhang, L., Marinov, D., Khurshid, S. (2013) "Faster mutation testing inspired by test prioritization and reduction", ISSTA. https://doi.org/10.1145/2483760.2483782 . Applies test prioritisation and reduction ideas to mutant execution, stopping on first kill. (†) Why it matters: early termination is what our "first divergent tic" naturally gives; this paper quantifies the saving.
- Zhang, J., Wang, Z., Zhang, L., Hao, D., Zang, L., Cheng, S., Zhang, L. (2016) "Predictive mutation testing", ISSTA. https://doi.org/10.1145/2931037.2931038 . Predicts per-mutant kill outcomes from static and dynamic features without running mutants (speedups reported up to about 150x). Follow-ups predict the per-test kill matrix: Kim, J., Jeon, J., Hong, S., Yoo, S. (2022) TOSEM https://doi.org/10.1145/3510417 (arXiv https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.10865); Jain, K., Alon, U., Groce, A., Le Goues, C. (2023) "Contextual Predictive Mutation Testing", arXiv https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.02389 . Why it matters: predicting phenotypes from code is the "can a model learn roles" counterpart of our assay; useful as an extra baseline.

### 1.3 Operator sufficiency, selection and sampling
- Offutt, A. J., Rothermel, G., Zapf, C. (1993) "An experimental evaluation of selective mutation", ICSE. https://doi.org/10.1109/icse.1993.346062 ; Offutt, A. J., Lee, A., Rothermel, G., Untch, R. H., Zapf, C. (1996) "An experimental determination of sufficient mutant operators", ACM TOSEM. https://doi.org/10.1145/227607.227610 . A small operator subset yields almost the same adequacy as the full set. Why it matters: justifies one-token operator families but also says the subset was calibrated for test adequacy, not for functional coverage.
- Wong, W. E., Mathur, A. P. (1995) "Reducing the cost of mutation testing: an empirical study", J. Systems and Software. https://doi.org/10.1016/0164-1212(94)00098-0 . Random sampling of mutants and constrained mutation. (†) Why it matters: the sampled-mutation baseline.
- Zhang, L., Hou, S.-S., Hu, J.-J., Xie, T., Mei, H. (2010) "Is operator-based mutant selection superior to random mutant selection?", ICSE. https://doi.org/10.1145/1806799.1806863 ; Zhang, L., Gligoric, M., Marinov, D., Khurshid, S. (2013) "Operator-based and random mutant selection: Better together", ASE. https://doi.org/10.1109/ase.2013.6693070 . Random selection is competitive with operator-based selection; combining the two is better. (†) Why it matters: supports a sampling-vs-full ablation with confidence intervals.
- Gopinath, R., Ahmed, I., Alipour, M. A., Jensen, C., Groce, A. (2017) "Mutation Reduction Strategies Considered Harmful", IEEE Trans. Reliability. https://doi.org/10.1109/tr.2017.2705662 . Argues many reduction strategies do not beat random selection of the same size. (†) Why it matters: do not over-engineer selection; compare to uniform random.
- Papadakis, M., Malevris, N. (2010) "An Empirical Evaluation of the First and Second Order Mutation Testing Strategies", ICSTW. https://doi.org/10.1109/icstw.2010.50 . Compares first-order mutation with second-order strategies. (†) Why it matters: the higher-order cost/benefit data point.
- Jia, Y., Harman, M. (2009) "Higher Order Mutation Testing", Information and Software Technology. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.infsof.2009.04.016 ; Jia, Y., Harman, M. (2008) "Constructing Subtle Faults Using Higher Order Mutation Testing", SCAM. https://doi.org/10.1109/scam.2008.36 . Higher-order mutants combine first-order ones; some are "subtle" (harder to kill than their parts). Why it matters: the software analogue of genetic interaction (see 5.3) and a route to a double-lesion screen.

### 1.4 Equivalent mutants and compiler-level equivalence
- Madeyski, L., Orzeszyna, W., Torkar, R., Jozala, M. (2014) "Overcoming the Equivalent Mutant Problem: A Systematic Literature Review and a Comparative Experiment of Second Order Mutation", IEEE TSE. https://doi.org/10.1109/tse.2013.44 . Reviews detection/avoidance techniques. (†)
- Papadakis, M., Jia, Y., Harman, M., Le Traon, Y. (2015) "Trivial Compiler Equivalence: A Large Scale Empirical Study of a Simple, Fast and Effective Equivalent Mutant Detection Technique", ICSE. https://doi.org/10.1109/icse.2015.103 ; Kintis, M., Papadakis, M., Jia, Y., Malevris, N., Le Traon, Y., Harman, M. (2018) "Detecting Trivial Mutant Equivalences via Compiler Optimisations", IEEE TSE. https://doi.org/10.1109/tse.2017.2684805 . Compare optimised binaries of original and mutant; identical code implies equivalent. Why it matters: a free, build-time filter for our C-family lesions (does not apply across JVM/.NET without adaptation).
- Kushigian, B., Kaufman, S. J., Featherman, R., Potter, H., Madadi, A., Just, R. (2024) "Equivalent Mutants in the Wild: Identifying and Efficiently Suppressing Equivalent Mutants for Java Programs", ISSTA. https://doi.org/10.1145/3650212.3680310 . Empirical study of real equivalent mutants and suppression rules. (†) Why it matters: catalogues which operator patterns are routinely equivalent.

### 1.5 Coupling effect and subsumption (mutant redundancy)
- DeMillo, R. A., Lipton, R. J., Sayward, F. G. (1978) "Hints on Test Data Selection: Help for the Practicing Programmer", IEEE Computer. https://doi.org/10.1109/c-m.1978.218136 . Origin of the competent-programmer and coupling-effect hypotheses. (†)
- Offutt, A. J. (1992) "Investigations of the software testing coupling effect", ACM TOSEM. https://doi.org/10.1145/125489.125473 . Empirical support: tests that kill simple mutants kill most complex ones. (†)
- Gopinath, R., Jensen, C., Groce, A. (2017) "The Theory of Composite Faults", ICST. https://doi.org/10.1109/icst.2017.12 . A probabilistic account of when the coupling effect holds. (†) Why it matters: tells us when single-lesion phenotypes predict multi-lesion ones.
- Ammann, P., Delamaro, M. E., Offutt, J. (2014) "Establishing Theoretical Minimal Sets of Mutants", ICST. https://doi.org/10.1109/icst.2014.13 ; Kurtz, B., Ammann, P., Delamaro, M. E., Offutt, J., Deng, L. (2014) "Mutant Subsumption Graphs", ICSTW. https://doi.org/10.1109/icstw.2014.20 . Mutant A subsumes B if every test killing A kills B; minimal sets remove redundancy. (†) Why it matters: phenotype similarity must be computed on subsumption-reduced or at least weighted sets.
- Shi, A., Bell, J., Marinov, D. (2019) "Mitigating the effects of flaky tests on mutation testing", ISSTA. https://doi.org/10.1145/3293882.3330568 . Flaky tests change mutant verdicts; reruns/filters needed. (†) Why it matters: even with deterministic demos, verify run-to-run stability.
- Hariri, F., Shi, A., Fernando, V., Mahmood, S., Marinov, D. (2019) "Comparing Mutation Testing at the Levels of Source Code and Compiler Intermediate Representation", ICST. https://doi.org/10.1109/icst.2019.00021 . Source-level and IR-level mutation scores correlate closely; IR (LLVM) mutation is cheaper. Denisov, A., Pankevich, S. (2018) "Mull It Over: Mutation Testing Based on LLVM", ICSTW. https://doi.org/10.1109/icstw.2018.00024 . Why it matters: IR-level lesions are a route to comparable operators across C-like ports.

## 2. Mutation-based fault localisation and failure patterns as fingerprints

- Papadakis, M., Le Traon, Y. (2013; print 2015) "Metallaxis-FL: mutation-based fault localization", Software Testing, Verification and Reliability 25. https://doi.org/10.1002/stvr.1509 . Ranks program places by how closely the failing-test pattern of mutants at that place resembles the failing-test pattern of the real fault, using SBFL-style formulas on kill sets. Why it matters: the clearest precedent for "the failure pattern a lesion causes characterises the code it touches"; ours compares lesion patterns across programs rather than lesion-to-fault inside one.
- Moon, S., Kim, Y., Kim, M., Yoo, S. (2014) "Ask the Mutants: Mutating Faulty Programs for Fault Localization" (MUSE), ICST. https://doi.org/10.1109/icst.2014.28 . Mutating a faulty statement tends to change failing tests to passing more than mutating a correct one. Why it matters: second formulation of the same signal, with a different statistic.
- Hong, S., Lee, B., Kwak, T., Jeon, Y., Ko, B., Kim, Y., Kim, M. (2015) "Mutation-Based Fault Localization for Real-World Multilingual Programs", ASE. https://doi.org/10.1109/ase.2015.14 . Extends MBFL to multilingual systems. (†) Why it matters: practical notes on mutating across language boundaries.
- Pearson, S., Campos, J., Just, R., Fraser, G., Abreu, R., Ernst, M. D., Pang, D., Keller, B. (2017) "Evaluating and Improving Fault Localization", ICSE. https://doi.org/10.1109/icse.2017.62 . Shows artificial-fault results do not always transfer to real faults; MBFL does well on real faults. (†) Why it matters: validity of evaluating with synthetic lesions.
- Kim, J., An, G., Feldt, R., Yoo, S. (2023) "Learning Test-Mutant Relationship for Accurate Fault Localisation" (SIMFL), arXiv https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.02319 (accepted to Information and Software Technology per the arXiv page). Learns a statistical model relating mutant test results to mutant locations from past versions, so no new mutation run is needed when a fault appears. Why it matters: kill matrix reused across versions of ONE project; the closest "transfer" precedent, still within a project.
- Shin, D., Bae, D.-H. (2016) "A Theoretical Framework for Understanding Mutation-Based Testing Methods", ICST (arXiv https://arxiv.org/abs/1601.06466 ; the arXiv page says it was to appear at ICST 2016). Defines "test differentiators" and d-vectors that record, per test, where two program variants behave differently, and uses them to interpret MBFL and mutant minimisation. Why it matters: gives us a formal vocabulary and distance for "phenotype vectors".
- Jones, J. A., Harrold, M. J., Stasko, J. (2002) "Visualization of test information to assist fault localization" (Tarantula), ICSE. https://doi.org/10.1109/icse.2002.1007991 ; Abreu, R., Zoeteweij, P., Golsteijn, R., van Gemund, A. J. C. (2009) "A practical evaluation of spectrum-based fault localization", J. Systems and Software. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jss.2009.06.035 ; Wong, W. E., Gao, R., Li, Y., Abreu, R., Wotawa, F. (2016) "A Survey on Software Fault Localization", IEEE TSE. https://doi.org/10.1109/tse.2016.2521368 . SBFL suspiciousness formulas (Tarantula, Ochiai) and the survey. Why it matters: ready-made similarity statistics over pass/fail vectors.
- Liblit, B., Naik, M., Zheng, A. X., Aiken, A., Jordan, M. I. (2005) "Scalable statistical bug isolation", PLDI. https://doi.org/10.1145/1064978.1065014 ; Baah, G. K., Podgurski, A., Harrold, M. J. (2010) "Causal inference for statistical fault localization", ISSTA. https://doi.org/10.1145/1831708.1831717 . Predicate-level statistical isolation and causal adjustment for confounding by coverage. (†) Why it matters: the confounder (coverage) problem is the same as "is the lesion reached by the demo".
- Zeller, A., Hildebrandt, R. (2002) "Simplifying and isolating failure-inducing input", IEEE TSE. https://doi.org/10.1109/32.988498 (delta debugging). (†) Why it matters: minimising the first divergent tic's cause; a possible follow-up for attributing divergence to the exact state field.
- Harrold, M. J., Rothermel, G., Wu, R., Yi, L. (1998) "An empirical investigation of program spectra", PASTE/SIGPLAN Notices. https://doi.org/10.1145/277633.277647 ; Reps, T., Ball, T., Das, M., Larus, J. (1997) "The use of program profiling for software maintenance with applications to the year 2000 problem", ESEC/FSE (SIGSOFT SEN). https://doi.org/10.1145/267896.267925 ; Ernst, M. D., Cockrell, J., Griswold, W. G., Notkin, D. (1999) "Dynamically discovering likely program invariants to support program evolution", ICSE. https://doi.org/10.1145/302405.302467 . Dynamic spectra and invariants as behavioural summaries of code. (†) Why it matters: older "behavioural fingerprint" tradition without perturbation.
- Perturbation without mutation: Voas, J. M. (1992) "PIE: a dynamic failure-based technique", IEEE TSE. https://doi.org/10.1109/32.153381 ; Hiller, M., Jhumka, A., Suri, N. (2004) "EPIC: profiling the propagation and effect of data errors in software", IEEE Trans. Computers. https://doi.org/10.1109/tc.2004.1275294 . Perturb internal state and measure propagation to outputs to rate how critical a location is. (†) Why it matters: closest "sensitivity of the output to damage at this place" idea; ranks locations but does not match them across programs.

## 3. Behavioural and dynamic similarity of code

### 3.1 Functional/semantic clones by execution
- Jiang, L., Su, Z. (2009) "Automatic mining of functionally equivalent code fragments via random testing", ISSTA. https://doi.org/10.1145/1572272.1572283 (record confirmed; abstract points per search snippets and the follow-up below). Randomly tests extracted code fragments and groups those with identical input-output behaviour (EqMiner); found many functionally equivalent but syntactically different fragments in the Linux kernel. Why it matters: the standard "behaviour not structure" precedent; behaviour here is I/O, not response to damage.
- Deissenboeck, F., Heinemann, L., Hummel, B., Wagner, S. (2012) "Challenges of the Dynamic Detection of Functionally Similar Code Fragments", CSMR (arXiv https://arxiv.org/abs/1801.06107 ). Applying the Jiang-Su idea to Java found far fewer matches and many practical obstacles. Why it matters: realistic expectations for dynamic matching.
- Elva, R., Leavens, G. T. (2012) "Semantic clone detection using method IOE-behavior", IWSC. https://doi.org/10.1109/iwsc.2012.6227874 . Characterises methods by input, output and effects; the effects part is relevant to state-mutating functions. (†)
- Su, F.-H., Bell, J., Harvey, K., Sethumadhavan, S., Kaiser, G., Jebara, T. (2016) "Code relatives: detecting similarly behaving software", FSE. https://doi.org/10.1145/2950290.2950321 . Finds code that behaves alike by comparing dynamic traces rather than text. (†)
- Wagner, S., Abdulkhaleq, A., Bogicevic, I., Ostberg, J.-P., Ramadani, J. (2016) "How are functionally similar code clones syntactically different? An empirical study and a benchmark", PeerJ preprint (published PeerJ Computer Science 2016; preprint DOI verified). https://doi.org/10.7287/peerj.preprints.1516v2 . Studies how independently written functionally similar code differs syntactically. Why it matters: direct precedent for "role conserved, structure diverged".
- Thaller, H., Linsbauer, L., Egyed, A. (2020) "Semantic Clone Detection via Probabilistic Software Modeling", arXiv https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.04891 . Models runtime behaviour probabilistically to measure behavioural distance between code elements. (†)
- Mathew, G., Stolee, K. T. (2021) "Cross-language code search using static and dynamic analyses", ESEC/FSE. https://doi.org/10.1145/3468264.3468538 . Finds equivalent code across languages by combining static and dynamic (I/O) signals. Why it matters: the nearest existing cross-language behavioural matcher; use as a baseline concept.
- Stolee, K. T., Elbaum, S., Dwyer, M. B. (2016) "Code search with input/output queries: Generalizing, ranking, and assessment", J. Systems and Software. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jss.2015.04.081 . Semantic code search by I/O examples with symbolic execution. (†)
- Schuler, D., Dallmeier, V., Lindig, C. (2007) "A dynamic birthmark for Java", ASE. https://doi.org/10.1145/1321631.1321672 ; Myles, G., Collberg, C. (2004) "Detecting Software Theft via Whole Program Path Birthmarks", ISC (LNCS). https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-540-30144-8_34 . Birthmarks identify a program by runtime-derived characteristics robust to code changes. (†) Why it matters: the closest conceptual cousin to "phenotype as an identity invariant under structural change".
- Carzaniga, A., Mattavelli, A., Pezze, M. (2015) "Measuring Software Redundancy", ICSE. https://doi.org/10.1109/icse.2015.37 . Defines redundancy as functional equivalence with diverse execution and measures it from execution traces. (†)

### 3.2 Cross-language clone detection
- Perez, D., Chiba, S. (2019) "Cross-Language Clone Detection by Learning Over Abstract Syntax Trees", MSR. https://doi.org/10.1109/msr.2019.00078 . Learns shared AST embeddings across languages (structure/lexical). (†)
- Nafi, K. W., Kar, T. S., Roy, B., Roy, C. K., Schneider, K. A. (2019) "CLCDSA: Cross Language Code Clone Detection using Syntactical Features and API Documentation", ASE. https://doi.org/10.1109/ase.2019.00099 . Syntactic features plus API documentation similarity. (†) Why it matters: shows cross-language methods lean on names/APIs, which our design deliberately removes.

### 3.3 Binary code similarity (structure vs behaviour; ground truth)
- Haq, I. U., Caballero, J. (2021) "A Survey of Binary Code Similarity", ACM Computing Surveys. https://doi.org/10.1145/3446371 . Taxonomy of syntactic, semantic and dynamic approaches. (†)
- Egele, M., Woo, M., Chapman, P., Brumley, D. (2014) "Blanket Execution: Dynamic Similarity Testing for Program Binaries and Components", USENIX Security. https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity14/technical-sessions/presentation/egele . Executes functions in a randomised environment, records side effects, and judges functions similar when effects match; beat BinDiff on cross-compiler/optimisation pairs. Why it matters: dynamic function matching with side-effect profiles; ground truth comes from compiling one source with different settings.
- Pewny, J., Garmany, B., Gawlik, R., Rossow, C., Holz, T. (2015) "Cross-Architecture Bug Search in Binary Executables", IEEE S&P. https://doi.org/10.1109/sp.2015.49 . Input/output behavioural sampling of IR to find buggy functions across architectures. (†)
- Xu, X., Liu, C., Feng, Q., Yin, H., Song, L., Song, D. (2017) "Neural Network-based Graph Embedding for Cross-Platform Binary Code Similarity Detection" (Gemini), CCS. https://doi.org/10.1145/3133956.3134018 ; Ding, S. H. H., Fung, B. C. M., Charland, P. (2019) "Asm2Vec", IEEE S&P. https://doi.org/10.1109/sp.2019.00003 . Learned static representations of control-flow graphs/assembly. Why it matters: the structural-learning counterpart to our fingerprint matcher.
- Marcelli, A., Graziano, M., Ugarte-Pedrero, X., Fratantonio, Y., Mansouri, M., Balzarotti, D. (2022) "How Machine Learning Is Solving the Binary Function Similarity Problem", USENIX Security. https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity22/presentation/marcelli . Re-implements and fairly compares many approaches on one dataset built from many compilers, optimisation levels and architectures. Why it matters: model for a fair multi-method comparison and for ground truth by construction (same source, different build).
- Kim, D., Kim, E., Cha, S. K., Son, S., Kim, Y. (2023) "Revisiting Binary Code Similarity Analysis Using Interpretable Feature Engineering and Lessons Learned", IEEE TSE. https://doi.org/10.1109/tse.2022.3187689 . Large benchmark (BinKit) and finding that simple features rival deep models. (†)
- Mapping across versions: Kim, M., Notkin, D. (2006) "Program element matching for multi-version program analyses", MSR. https://doi.org/10.1145/1137983.1137999 ; Zhang, X., Gupta, R. (2005) "Matching execution histories of program versions", ESEC/FSE (SIGSOFT SEN). https://doi.org/10.1145/1095430.1081738 . Match functions across versions by names/structure or by dynamic histories. Why it matters: the matching problem formulation we inherit, without mutation.

## Risks and lessons from the literature

1. Equivalent and unreached mutants. A lesion with no phenotype may be equivalent, unreached by any demo, or masked; these are different things (Madeyski 2014; Papadakis 2015 TCE; Kushigian 2024). Record per-lesion coverage ("was the mutated line executed in any demo?") and treat unreached as missing data, not as "role: none". Expect roughly a third of random mutations to be neutral under the tests (Schulte 2014).
2. Mutant dependence and clustering. Mutants are not independent: subsumed/duplicate mutants inflate counts (Kurtz 2014; Ammann 2014; Papadakis 2016). Use cluster-robust resampling by function/line, and weight or deduplicate identical phenotypes before computing similarity.
3. Selection bias of killed mutants. Phenotypes are only observable for killed mutants and depend on which demos exist; compare only on a shared demo set and report the fraction of lesions with any phenotype per function (Just 2014 shows many real faults are not coupled to mutants).
4. Test-suite power and coverage confounding. Coverage is a poor stand-in for suite strength (Inozemtseva and Holmes, https://doi.org/10.1145/2568225.2568271 ; Gopinath et al., https://doi.org/10.1145/2568225.2568278 ). Statistical fault-localisation work stresses confounding by coverage (Baah 2010): functions executed by many demos look alike. Normalise for coverage and use IDF-style weights on demos (rare phenotypes count more), as phenologs do with a hypergeometric test.
5. Nondeterminism and flakiness. Verify identical digests over repeated runs of the unmutated build on every demo, and across hosts/compilers (Fiedler: floating point and build modes; Shi 2019 on flaky tests altering verdicts).
6. Undefined behaviour and run-away mutants. C lesions can introduce undefined behaviour (compiler-dependent outcomes), hangs and crashes; time-outs need a policy, and crashes vs divergence should be separate phenotype classes. LLVM-level tools such as Mull exist (Denisov 2018); source-vs-IR levels give similar scores (Hariri 2019). Different languages overflow, shift and divide differently, so the "same" lesion is not the same event in every port; stratify by operator family.
7. Coupling effect. Single-token lesions represent harder faults only under the coupling hypothesis (Offutt 1992; Gopinath 2017); we use lesions as probes, not as models of real faults, so claims should be about probes and not about fault-detection.
8. Operator sufficiency. Operator sets were validated for adequacy, not for coverage of function roles (Offutt 1996); different sets could give different roles. Report sensitivity to dropping each operator family.
9. Ground truth in similarity benchmarks. Heuristic or name-based labels contaminate semantic evaluation (Krinke and Ragkhitwetsagul 2022, 2025). In our setting name matches are a silver standard that shares information with the structural matcher; hold out a hand-audited sample.
10. Orthology semantics. Phenotype match does not imply common descent or one-to-one correspondence (Nehrt 2011; Gabaldon and Koonin 2013): split, merged and inlined functions are one-to-many. Allow many-to-many matching and test whether sibling functions inside one port out-match cross-port counterparts.
11. Independence of ports. Independent implementations share hard spots (Knight and Leveson 1986) and many DOOM ports derive from common code; shared ancestry can look like role conservation. Stratify pairs by lineage depth (descended-from vs rewritten).
12. Generalisation of the reference. As in Chekam 2017, do not treat the reference engine as perfect; digests compare ports to each other, not to ground truth.
13. Digest granularity. The state-field attribution depends on what the digest hashes; two functions may differ in phenotype only because they touch fields not hashed. State the observable set explicitly and test sensitivity to it.

## Novelty assessment

**Claim A: function roles defined by lesion phenotype and conserved across independent implementations.** Status: partially exists (components), not found as a whole. Evidence: kill-pattern-as-signature exists inside one program for fault localisation (Metallaxis, MUSE, SIMFL; d-vector theory); behavioural cross-language/binary function matching exists but uses input-output or traces, not damage response (Jiang and Su 2009, BLEX 2014, code relatives 2016, Mathew and Stolee 2021); cross-language mutation exists for translator evaluation (Guizzo 2023); perturbation-sensitivity profiling exists as PIE/EPIC. No paper found that matches functions across programs by their mutant-response profile. Search terms used: mutation function role cross-program; mutant kill vector signature compare functions; mutation analysis infer function semantics; semantic clone mutant; fault injection function matching role; differential mutation; mutation-based code similarity; kill matrix transferable across programs.

**Claim B: mutant schemata as a phenotyping assay (one build, environment-variable switches, replay a demo suite, record which demos/tic/field).** Status: partially exists. Evidence: schemata (Untch 1993; Major 2014) are for efficient adequacy scoring; kill matrices are used for fault localisation and prediction (Kim 2022; Jain 2023); first-divergence/early termination is standard (Zhang 2013). Not found: schemata or kill matrices recorded as a descriptive, multi-observable assay of function behaviour (tic and state-field resolution rather than boolean kill). The closest biological term is deep mutational scanning (Fowler and Fields 2014). Search terms: mutant schemata; meta-mutant; mutation analysis as assay; kill matrix representation; saturation mutagenesis source code.

**Claim C: comparing structure-based and phenotype-based orthology (and how structure, size and names diverged while role was conserved).** Status: partially exists. Evidence: clone literature explicitly compares syntactic with behavioural similarity and finds functionally similar code often syntactically different (Wagner 2016; Deissenboeck 2012; Jiang and Su 2009); binary-similarity studies compare structural vs dynamic matchers (Egele 2014; Marcelli 2022). Biology provides the comparison paradigm (Altenhoff 2016 benchmark; Nehrt 2011). Not found: a cross-implementation study of DOOM or any game engine doing this, nor any using lesion phenotypes as the behavioural side. Search terms: orthology software functions ports; software phylogenetics orthologs paralogs; DOOM source ports empirical study; functionally similar clones syntactically different; ortholog conjecture software.

Caveat on all three: the search covered web indexes, Crossref and arXiv but not Google Scholar, ACM DL or DBLP search, so a very recent or obscure workshop paper could exist. A "not found" is therefore a moderate-confidence statement.

## Suggested baselines and metrics to adopt

1. Phenotype similarity: Jaccard, Ochiai and d-vector Hamming distance over (lesion class x demo) outcomes; a phenolog-style hypergeometric/Fisher test for overlap of broken-demo sets with IDF weighting; add first-divergence-tic and state-field agreement as separate scores.
2. Matching: reciprocal best hits (Moreno-Hagelsieb) plus OrthoMCL-style clustering on the phenotype graph; one-to-many allowed.
3. Controls: size-matched, call-depth-matched and same-file random pairs; within-port sibling pairs (ortholog-conjecture test); shuffled-demo and shuffled-lesion nulls; report effect sizes with cluster-bootstrap CIs.
4. Mutant hygiene: per-lesion coverage flag; TCE-style equivalence filter where compilers allow; subsumption/duplicate collapsing; repeat-run stability check; separate crash, hang and silent-divergence classes.
5. Cost ablations: uniform random sampling at fixed rates vs full set (Zhang 2010/2013; Gopinath 2017) to show phenotype stability under sampling; leave-one-demo-out and operator-family-drop sensitivity.
6. Orthology benchmarking in the Altenhoff style: score structural, phenotype, name and combined matchers against several independent proxies (hand audits, translated-from lineage where known, call-graph neighbourhood agreement), not one gold standard.
7. Behavioural-matcher baseline: a simple I/O or trace-signature matcher in the spirit of Jiang-Su / Mathew-Stolee / BLEX to show what the lesion response adds over plain behaviour.
8. Fault-localisation statistics reused as scoring: Metallaxis-style max/avg suspiciousness mapped to "role similarity" for pairs, plus Tarantula/Ochiai variants.
9. Optional learned baseline: predict a function's phenotype from code (Kim 2022; Jain 2023 style) as an upper bound on how much of the role is recoverable from structure/names.
